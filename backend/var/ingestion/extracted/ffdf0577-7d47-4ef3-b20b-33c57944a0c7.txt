PART A – GROUP I: APPEALS OF THE SRA ARISING OUT OF THE 
JUDGMENT DATED 05.01.2022 
 
3. The appeals in this group raise questions of considerable importance 
in the working of the Code, namely, (i) the nature and limits of the 
jurisdiction of the NCLT and the NCLAT while dealing with a resolution 
plan, (ii) whether a resolution plan which provides for payment to 
dissenting financial creditors by way of non-convertible debentures 
satisfies the requirement contained in Section 30(2)(b) of the Code, 
and (iii) whether the CoC, having once approved a resolution plan 
under Section 30(4) of the Code, can revisit its decision while the 
approval is subject to challenge in a statutory appeal? 
FACTUAL BACKGROUND 
 
4. The facts giving rise to the filing of these appeals, briefly stated, are 
that, the applications under Section 7 of the Code were filed by the 
State Bank of India (‘SBI’) and other creditors against thirteen 
companies of the Videocon group, including VIL. The said applications 
were admitted by the NCLT by various orders passed between 
06.06.2018 and 25.09.2018. The NCLT, Mumbai, on 08.08.2019 
directed consolidation of the CIRPs of the thirteen corporate debtors 
(‘the corporate debtors’). The Resolution Professional (‘RP’) invited 
expressions of interest on 11.10.2019. On 26.11.2019, the RP issued 
the Information Memorandum along with request for resolution plans.

5 
 
The timeline for submission of resolution plans was extended on 
several occasions. 
5. The RP received eleven resolution plans, which were opened in the 15th 
meeting of the CoC held on 02.09.2020. At that meeting, the CoC was 
informed that the fair value and the liquidation value of the corporate 
debtors, being the average of the estimates provided by two registered 
valuers, were Rs. 4,069.95 crores and Rs. 2,568.13 crores 
respectively. Two of the resolution plans, namely those submitted by 
the SRA and V Shape Investment Management Limited, were found to 
be compliant and were placed before the CoC. A proposal for 
withdrawal of the CIRP under Section 12A of the Code was also 
submitted by the Promoter on 31.08.2020. The said proposal was 
placed before the CoC for its consideration. 
6. On 07.11.2020, the SRA submitted an amended and restated 
resolution plan with a total financial outlay of Rs. 2,962.02 crores. 
The resolution plan provided, inter alia, for (i) an upfront payment of 
Rs. 200 crores to the financial creditors; (ii) an upfront payment of         
Rs. 52 crores towards the admitted dues of workmen and employees; 
(iii) an upfront payment of Rs. 10 crores to the operational creditors; 
(iv) issuance of non-convertible debentures (‘NCDs’) of an aggregate 
face value of Rs. 2,700 crores, carrying an annual coupon of 6.65 per

6 
 
cent, to the financial creditors, redeemable in five instalments, (v) the 
first instalment of Rs. 200 crores falling due 25 months after the 
closing date and the remaining four of Rs. 625 crores each falling due 
in the third to sixth years; (vi) 8 per cent equity in VIL to the financial 
creditors; and (vii) the cash balances of the corporate debtors to accrue 
to the financial creditors. Clause 3.5.1 of the resolution plan provided 
that dissenting financial creditors would not be paid less than the 
amount payable under Section 53 of the Code and would be paid in 
priority as per Regulation 38(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy 
Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) 
Regulations, 2016 (‘CIRP Regulations’). Clause 3.5.7 of the resolution 
plan provided that “in all circumstances and in any event” payment to 
dissenting financial creditors would be made strictly as per Section 
30(2)(b) of the Code and Regulation 38(1)(b) of CIRP Regulations. 
7. In the 19th meeting of the CoC held on 11.11.2020, it was resolved 
that the two compliant plans and the proposal under Section 12A 
would be put to vote simultaneously. On 13.11.2020, SBI Capital 
Markets Limited (‘SBI Caps’), the process advisor to the CoC, 
circulated a distribution calculation according to which the dissenting 
financial creditors, if any, would receive Rs. 114.21 crores. Upon 
conclusion of the voting on 11.12.2020, the Plan of the SRA was

per cent of the voting share. On 12.12.2020, the RP issued a Letter of

performance bank guarantee of Rs. 296.20 crores.

Maharashtra filed I.A. No. 982 of 2021 objecting to approval of the 
resolution plan on the grounds that it provided for payment to 
dissenting financial creditors in NCDs and that there was a

ORDER OF THE NCLT 
 
9. By an order dated 08.06.2021, the NCLT held as follows: (i) out of 
admitted claims of Rs. 64,838.63 crores, the Plan offered Rs. 2,962.02 
crores, entailing a haircut of 95.85 per cent, (ii) the operational 
creditors would receive 0.72 per cent of their admitted claims, and (iii) 
by paying Rs. 262 crores the SRA would obtain possession of all the 
thirteen corporate debtors. The NCLT, having deference to the 
commercial wisdom of the CoC, approved the plan under Section 31(1) 
of the Code. The NCLT, however, requested the CoC and the SRA to 
increase the payout to operational creditors, particularly MSMEs. The 
NCLT directed that the dissenting financial creditors be paid their 
liquidation value in cash, upfront, before any payment is made to the 
assenting financial creditors. 
10. The order of approval was challenged before the NCLAT by Bank of 
Maharashtra, IFCI Limited and SIDBI by way of appeals, namely,

Company Appeal (AT) (Ins.) No. 545 of 2021. The appeal of the 
Promoter was registered as Company Appeal (AT) (Ins.) No. 650 of 
2021.  
11. On 19.07.2021, the NCLAT, at the stage of issuance of notice, noted 
the manner of payment proposed under the Plan to the dissenting 
financial creditors, that is, deferment of the bulk of the amount in 
NCDs which was redeemable over a period of six years. Thereafter, 
considering the observations of the Adjudicating Authority and the 
“exceptional facts” of the matter, the NCLAT stayed the order of 
approval and directed status quo ante to be maintained. The SRA’s 
challenge to the said order was disposed of by this Court on 
13.08.2021 with a request to the NCLAT to decide the appeals 
expeditiously. 
12. By an affidavit dated 17.09.2021, filed on 18.09.2021, the CoC, 
through SBI on behalf of the assenting financial creditors constituting 
about 94.98 per cent of the voting share, informed the NCLAT that, in 
view of the observations of the Adjudicating Authority and the NCLAT,

10

purpose. 
JUDGMENT OF NCLAT 
13. By the impugned judgment dated 05.01.2022, the NCLAT held, inter 
alia, that (i) the resolution plan did not comply with Section 30(2)(b) 
of the Code, inasmuch as payment to the dissenting financial creditors 
by way of NCDs redeemable after a long period did not qualify as 
“payment” in accordance of order of priority; (ii) the direction of the 
NCLT to pay the dissenting financial creditors in cash amounted to a 
modification of the resolution plan, which lay within the domain of the 
CoC and not of the Adjudicating Authority; (iii) the CoC, which has the 
power to approve a plan, has also the power to reconsider its decision 
and is not functus officio upon approval of the Plan; and (iv) prior 
approval of the Competition Commission of India (‘CCI’) under the 
proviso to Section 31(4) of the Code was not shown to have been 
obtained.  
14. The NCLAT accordingly set aside the approval of the Plan by the CoC 
and by the Adjudicating Authority and remitted the matter to the CoC 
for completion of the CIRP in accordance with the Code. By the same 
judgment, the NCLAT dismissed the appeal of the Promoter, holding

11 
 
that his grievances regarding the haircut and the non-inclusion of the 
foreign oil and gas assets related to the commercial wisdom of the 
CoC, and noting that his Section 12A proposal had been rejected by 
the CoC by 98.14 per cent of the voting share.  
15. Pursuant to the impugned judgment, the CoC in its 25th meeting held 
on 12.01.2022 resolved by 97 per cent of the voting share to invite 
fresh expressions of interest, and a Form G was published on 
18.01.2022. When the appeals were taken up on 14.02.2022, this 
Court issued notice and the CoC was asked not to proceed further.

IN RE: ISSUE (ii) 
POWER OF NCLAT TO REMIT THE RESOLUTION PLAN TO CoC 
 
25. It is well-settled that an express grant of a statutory power carries with 
it, by necessary implication, every incidental and ancillary power 
required for its effective exercise.22 In the scheme of the Code, the power 
to remit a plan to CoC is not only permissible but is the only lawful 
course for the following reasons, (i) neither the adjudicating authority 
nor the NCLAT can modify a resolution plan and also cannot substitute 
their own commercial judgment for that of the CoC,  and (ii) rejecting a 
plan under Section 31(2) of the Code may, by virtue of Section 33(1)(b), 
lead to the liquidation of a corporate debtor, which the Code treats as a 
measure of last resort. The appellate power conferred on the NCLAT by 
necessary implication includes the power to remit a plan. The aforesaid 
inference is further fortified by Rule 11 of the National Company Law 
Appellate Tribunal Rules, 2016, which preserves the inherent powers of 
the Appellate Tribunal to make such orders or to give such directions as 
may be necessary for meeting the ends of justice or to prevent the abuse 
of the process of Appellate Tribunal.

for the following reasons, (i) once the resolution plan is found non-
compliant and its approval is set aside, the matter reverts to the CoC,

directed not to be treated as a precedent.   
26.3 It is noteworthy that the Code was amended by the Insolvency and

appropriate course is to send it back to the CoC for rectification, rather 
than to reject it outright or modify it. Our conclusion in the present 
case rests on the law as it then stood. The amendment, however, 
confirms the legislative policy that the remedy for a defective resolution 
plan lies in remittance to the CoC, and the NCLAT’s order of remittance 
is in consonance with it.

Regulations.

IN RE: ISSUE (iv)(a) 
ROLE OF CoC DURING CIRP AND WHETHER CoC HAD BECOME 
FUNCTUS OFFICIO 
 
29. The CoC is constituted under Section 21(1) of the Code and remains in 
existence throughout the CIRP. A majority of its members are public 
sector banks and financial institutions dealing with public money. The 
CoC, therefore, acts as custodian of public trust, discharges a statutory 
role and owes a duty of trust and care and its powers under the Code 
are commensurate with those responsibilities. The CoC is enjoined by 
the Code to choose the best resolution plan from the market and 
provide for any measure as part of such plan to ensure sustained life 
of the corporate debtor. The CoC is required to follow due process and 
be fair to all stakeholders and has to act in a transparent manner in 
discharge of its responsibilities. The objective of the Code is to respect 
the commercial wisdom of CoC.23 The appropriate disposition of a 
 
23 Report of the Insolvency Law Committee (March 2018)

41 
 
defaulting firm is a business decision and only the creditors should 
make it.24 
29.1 The doctrine of functus officio applies to an authority which, having 
discharged its function by a final decision, stands divested of further 
jurisdiction in the matter. The CoC is not an adjudicatory authority. It 
is a body of creditors, comprising domain experts, entrusted by the 
Code with the commercial decision on the viability and feasibility of a 
resolution plan. Its decisions are founded on its commercial wisdom, 
which this Court has repeatedly held to be paramount. The CoC does 
not cease to exist upon approving a plan. A three-Judge bench of this 
Court in Kalyani Transco (supra), rejected the contention that the 
CoC becomes functus officio upon approval of the resolution plan and 
held that a resolution plan approved under Section 31 does not attain 
finality until the appeals under Sections 61 and 62 are decided. It was 
further held that, in view of the Explanation to Regulation 18(2) of the 
CIRP Regulations, the CoC continues to exist, until the plan is 
implemented or an order of liquidation is passed. It was also held that 
in case after approval of resolution plan the CoC is held to be functus 
officio, the same would leave creditors without remedy, where a plan 
 
24 Report of the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee, Volume I: Rationale and Design 
(November 2015)

cannot be implemented or is set aside in appeal. Accordingly, issue no. 
(iv)(a) is answered in the negative.  
IN RE: ISSUE (iv)(b)  
CoC’s POWER TO REVISIT ITS DECISION  
 
29.2 In the present case, the approval of the resolution plan never attained 
finality. Within a little over a month of its approval, the order of the 
NCLT was assailed in statutory appeals under Section 61(3), and on 
19.07.2021, the NCLAT, citing the exceptional facts of the case, stayed 
the approval and directed maintenance of status quo ante. Such an 
order is unusual. Its effect was to restore the parties to the position 
existing before the order of approval and to keep the process open while 
the appeals were pending. It was in these circumstances, and after the 
interim order, that the CoC, by an overwhelming majority of about 95 
per cent, sought remission of the resolution plan for its reconsideration. 
The submission that the interim order did not confer on the CoC a carte 
blanche to revisit its commercial wisdom misses the point. The source 
of the CoC’s power is not only the interim order, but also its continuing 
role under the Code. The interim order merely ensured that the process 
was not foreclosed while the legality of the Plan was under scrutiny in 
the statutory appellate mechanism.

43 
 
29.3 The reliance placed by the SRA on Ebix (supra) and Amtek Auto 
(supra) is misplaced. In Ebix (supra), the question was whether a 
successful resolution applicant could withdraw or seek modification of 
a resolution plan after its approval by the CoC and while the application 
for approval was pending before the Adjudicating Authority. This Court 
held that the Code does not envisage such withdrawal, and that the 
binding effect of the plan inter se the CoC and the resolution applicant 
prevents either from reneging on its terms. Amtek Auto (supra) 
likewise concerned the resolution applicant’s attempt to walk out of an 
approved plan. Neither decision dealt with a situation where the 
approval of the plan by the Adjudicating Authority had been stayed and 
set aside in a statutory appeal on a ground under Section 61(3) of the 
Code, and the matter had reverted to the CoC. The binding character 
of a plan approved by the CoC presupposes a plan that is compliant 
with the Code. It cannot be invoked to insulate from reconsideration a 
plan which has been found to contravene Section 30(2)(b) of the Code.  
29.4 Likewise, K.N. Rajakumar (supra) arose in the context of a withdrawal 
under Section 12A and is of no assistance to the SRA. Similarly, the 
reliance on Kalpraj Dharamshi (supra) is also misconceived, since 
that decision affirmed the primacy of the commercial wisdom of the 
CoC, which in the present case, has been exercised in favour of

44 
 
reconsideration. Insofar as S.M. Kamal Pasha (supra) is concerned, it 
is noteworthy that, the CoC, on its own, sought to withdraw its approval 
of a plan which complied with the Code, solely because the financial 
position of the corporate debtor had improved. This Court termed that 
attempt “shortsighted opportunism” and not an exercise of commercial 
wisdom. Even so, it recognised that reconsideration may be directed to 
ensure compliance with Section 30(2) of the Code. In the present case, 
the resolution plan was found to contravene Section 30(2)(b) of the 
Code and its approval was set aside in a statutory appeal. The decision, 
therefore, does not assist the SRA. Therefore, the CoC was well within 
its right to revisit the decision. Thus, issue no. (iv)(b) is answered in the 
affirmative.

ANALYSIS 
 
45. The principal question which arises for consideration in this group is 
whether the foreign oil and gas assets of the Videocon group, which are

55 
 
held through the subsidiaries of VIL, can be included in the CIRP of 
VIL. 
46. A company may, for tax or regulatory reasons, divide itself into 
multitude of commonly owned subsidiaries. The Companies Act 
statutorily recognises subsidiary company as a separate legal entity. 
The holding company and the subsidiary company are considered as 
separate legal entities. The legal relationship between a holding and a 
subsidiary company is that they are distinct legal persons and a 
holding company does not own the assets of the subsidiary and in law 
the management of the business of the subsidiary also vests in its 
Board of Directors.30  
47. Section 18 of the Code requires the interim resolution professional to 
take control and custody of the assets over which the corporate debtor 
has ownership rights. The Explanation to Section 18 reads as under: 
“Explanation.—For the purposes of this section, 
the term “assets” shall not include the following, 
namely:— 
(a) assets owned by a third party in possession 
of the corporate debtor held under trust or 
under 
contractual 
arrangements 
including 
bailment; 
(b) assets of any Indian or foreign subsidiary of 
the corporate debtor; and 
 
30 Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India & Anr., (2012) 6 SCC 613

56 
 
(c) such other assets as may be notified by the 
Central Government in consultation with any 
financial sector regulator.” 
(emphasis supplied) 
 
48. The same legislative policy is reflected in Section 36(4)(d) of the Code, 
which excludes the assets of any Indian or foreign subsidiary of the 
corporate debtor from the liquidation estate. These provisions recognise 
the settled principle that a holding company and its subsidiary, even a 
wholly owned one, are distinct legal persons and that the holding 
company does not own the assets of its subsidiary.  
49. In the backdrop of aforesaid statutory provisions and the decision of 
this Court in Vodafone International Holdings B.V. (supra), we may 
advert to the facts of the case in hand. In December, 2005, VIL signed 
an MoU with BPCL to jointly acquire domestic and international oil and 
gas assets in Brazil and Indonesia. In pursuance of the aforesaid MoU, 
VIL and Bharat Petroleum Ventures BV which is a wholly owned 
subsidiary company of BPCL, formed a joint venture company in Brazil, 
namely, VB (Brasil) Petroleo Ltda (‘VB Brasil’). In July, 2009, VIL and 
BPRL entered into a Joint Study and Participation Agreement to jointly 
bid for an Indonesian Company, pursuant to which VINI was 
incorporated to hold the Indonesian assets. Thereafter, VIL transferred 
its entire quota in VB Brasil, later renamed IBV, to VEBL, its wholly 
owned subsidiary, which in turn came to be held through VHHL. VIL

57 
 
also entered into Beneficial Ownership Agreements with its 
downstream subsidiary companies. The aforesaid facts clearly 
demonstrate that the Brazilian and Indonesian assets are held by the 
subsidiaries of the VIL and not VIL itself. VIL does not hold any interest 
directly in the Brazilian and Indonesian assets but through its 
subsidiaries, namely, VOVL and VHHL. As already noticed supra, 
holding and subsidiary companies are separate and distinct legal 
entities, and the holding company does not own the assets of the 
subsidiary company. Therefore, even though VOVL, namely subsidiary 
of VIL, may have interest in Brazilian and Indonesian assets, the same 
do not become the assets of VIL. A plain reading of clause (b) of 
Explanation to Section 18, also supports the conclusion that the said 
assets cannot be termed as assets belonging to VIL. 
50. It is trite law that a party cannot be permitted to approbate and 
reprobate, or to blow hot and cold, by taking inconsistent stands to the 
prejudice of others; having accepted the benefit of, or acquiesced in, a 
particular course, he is estopped from repudiating it at a later stage.31 
51. In the instant case, the Promoter cannot be permitted to agitate the 
issue of inclusion of foreign oil and gas assets in the CIRP for the

pertinent to note that in Alpha Corp. (supra), the companies whose

parent company and had no assets other than the lands leased to them 
by the development authority for the very projects. The decision in LIC

The corporate veil is lifted to prevent fraud or the evasion of a statute, 
whereas here the statute itself, by Explanation (b) to Section 18, 
excludes the assets of a subsidiary, and the Explanation admits of no 
qualification as to the source from which the subsidiary acquired the 
assets.

resting on its commercial wisdom32 and no ground is made out to 
interfere with the same.

in the absence of any specific pleading describing particulars of fraud 
allegedly practised on the Adjudicating Authority. 
59. Now, we may deal with the submission made by BPRL that the CIRP of 
VOVL should not be interfered with. In the CIRP of VOVL, what was 
approved was the transfer of the quotas of VEBL in IBV, which stood 
pledged to the secured financial creditors of VOVL, pursuant to the 
exercise by BPRL of its pre-existing contractual ROFR under the QHA. 
The transaction did not involve treating the assets of VOVL’s 
subsidiaries as forming part of the estate of VOVL. In any event, the 
challenge of the Promoter to the transaction rests entirely on the 
premise that the assets belong to VIL, which we have already rejected. 
The transaction was approved by the CoC of VOVL by 99.96 per cent of 
the voting share in its commercial wisdom, and by the NCLT. The 
Promoter has no locus to question it. The transaction has since been 
concluded, and BPRL, a third party, has acted upon it, including by 
funding the defaults of VEBL for several years to preserve the assets. 
No ground is made out to interfere with the CIRP of VOVL.

63 
 
APPEAL OF THE VIL VENDORS ASSOCIATION 
 
60. The grievance of the VIL Vendors Association is derivative. The 
operational creditors of VIL are entitled to recover their dues from the 
estate of VIL in accordance with the Code. Since the foreign oil and gas 
assets do not form part of that estate, their exclusion causes no legal 
injury to them. The minutes of the meetings of the lenders relied upon 
relate to the enforcement of the pledge over the shares of VOVL, which 
was a pre-insolvency transaction; a challenge to such a transaction, if 
any, lies only in the manner provided under the Code before the 
Adjudicating Authority. If VIL parted with its rights or furnished the 
guarantee without consideration, the remedy lies under Sections 43 to 
51 and 66 of the Code before the Adjudicating Authority, and no such 
application has been filed. The inclusion of assets in the CIRP is a 
question of law under Section 18, and not of the commercial wisdom of 
the CoC. The valuation of assets which do not belong to VIL is, 
therefore, irrelevant. However, having regard to the nature of the issue, 
we grant permission to the VIL Vendors Association to pursue such 
remedies as may be available to it under law. 
CONSIDERATIONS WHICH OUGHT TO GUIDE NCLAT WHILE 
REMANDING AN APPROVED RESOLUTION PLAN

64 
 
61. Before parting with the judgment, we would like to advert to the scope 
and ambit of power of NCLAT to remand an approved resolution plan 
to the CoC.  
62. NCLAT, while exercising powers under Section 61(3) of the Code, may 
remand a non-compliant Resolution Plan back to the CoC to achieve 
the object and purpose of the Code. At the same time, we place 
guardrails on the power of remand, such that it is only used in the most 
appropriate situations.   
63. One of the elementary rules of interpretation is that a statute must be 
construed as a whole, in line with the object and intention of the 
Legislature, so as to achieve the purpose of the Statute.33 No provision 
is to be read in isolation, as a watertight compartment.34 The provisions 
must instead be read together so as to advance the legal and policy 
goals that the statute as a whole seeks to achieve. Therefore, our 
inquiry flows in the following manner: the power of remand cannot be 
assessed by looking at the appellate jurisdiction of the NCLAT in

64. Four sets of functionaries bear on the completion of the CIRP in the 
scheme of the Code : the RP to commence, conduct and conclude the 
process, the CoC to take commercial decisions, the Adjudicating 
Authority (NCLT) to examine whether decisions are in compliance 
under Section 31; and the Appellate Authority (NCLAT) under Sections 
61 and 62, to correct errors in that examination. While the role of each 
functionary is earmarked and operates at a distinctive stage, they are 
not watertight compartments; rather, they operate in tandem within 
the same framework so as to realise the ultimate purpose of maximising 
the value of assets, and to promote enterprise. These functionaries 
weave themselves into each other to realise the objective purpose of the 
insolvency resolution.  
65. The objectives of the Code are, first, to keep the corporate debtor a going 
concern; second, to provide an efficient recovery mechanism for all 
creditors; and third, to maximise the value of assets during insolvency 
in a time-bound manner.35 Underlying each of these is the object of 
preventing the liquidation of the corporate debtor, a measure the

66. It is against this yardstick that the necessity for the NCLAT to remand 
the approved resolution plan to the CoC must be examined. That such 
a power exists has already been recognised by this Court.37 What 
requires articulation is when the exercise of such power is necessary, 
and within what limits. 
67. The necessity becomes apparent from the structure of the appellate 
remedy itself. An appeal against an order approving a resolution plan 
lies only on the grounds enumerated in Section 61(3), and the NCLAT, 
confined to those grounds, may either dismiss the appeal or allow it. If 
the appeal is allowed, the only consequence, absent a remand, is that 
the approval is set aside. Where the CIRP period has run its course, 
such setting aside would ordinarily push the corporate debtor towards 
liquidation, the very outcome the Legislature has sought to avoid. A 
binary choice between affirming a non-compliant plan and setting it 
aside altogether would thus place the Appellate Authority at odds with 
the objectives it is meant to serve.

68. Remand resolves this dilemma and does so in a manner consistent with 
the allocation of roles among the actors under the Code. Where the 
defect calls for a modification to the commerciality of the plan itself, it 
would not be open to either the Adjudicating Authority or the Appellate 
Authority to undertake that modification, given that it is settled that 
the commercial wisdom of the CoC is paramount.38 The appropriate 
course is to remit the matter to the CoC, which alone is competent to 
reconsider the plan on its merits. The power of remand, therefore, 
operates as the connecting link between the three actors: it permits the 
appellate authority to check the legality without trespassing on the 
commerciality, which is the sole domain of the CoC, and keeps the Code 
functioning as a single organism, with each sub-unit serving its 
respective objective, rather than as watertight compartments. 
69. The same feature that makes remand useful, however, also makes it 
necessary to define its limits. Remand returns the matter to the body 
best placed to assess the merits, whether by reason of its domain 
expertise or its closer engagement with the facts. But it carries a clear 
cost: prolonged litigation, erosion of certainty, increased financial 
burden on the parties, and, in the context of Code, depletion of asset

secures its benefit by incurring the least potential costs. Since 
timeliness is itself an objective of the Code, the following considerations 
ought to guide the NCLAT when remanding an approved resolution 
plan to the CoC : 
a) The power of remand is to be exercised only in furtherance 
of the objectives of the Code, namely, keeping the corporate 
debtor a going concern, ensuring a fair and reasonable 
recovery for all classes of creditors, and maximising the 
value of the corporate debtor’s assets during the CIRP. A 
remand that advances none of these objectives is not 
justified merely because the power to order it exists. 
b) Remand must rest on a finding of legal non-compliance, 
whether under Section 30(2) or under any other law within
